A basis is a coordinate system, nothing more

A basis for a vector space is any set of vectors you can combine (with the right coefficients) to build every vector in the space. An orthonormal basis additionally has every pair of basis vectors at a right angle (inner product 0) and each one normalized (inner product with itself = 1) — exactly the two properties bra-ket's ⟨φ|ψ⟩ lets you check directly.

You've already used two different orthonormal bases without the label: the computational basis {|0⟩, |1⟩}, and the Hadamard basis {|+⟩, |−⟩}. "Hilbert space" is just the name for the (possibly infinite-dimensional, but 2-dimensional for one qubit) complex vector space all of this lives in — the word sounds heavier than the idea is.

Any single quantum state is basis-independent — it's a physical thing. The coefficients you write down for it change completely depending which basis you chose, the same way a compass bearing changes if you rotate your map's north.

Express one state in both bases

Build a state, then read off its coefficients in the Z basis and the X basis side by side — same physical arrow, two different coordinate readouts. We'll also verify each basis really is orthonormal, live.

SANDBOX · BUILD |ψ⟩
In the Z basis {|0⟩,|1⟩}
c₀=1.00, c₁=0.00
In the X basis {|+⟩,|−⟩}
d₀=0.71, d₁=0.71
Orthonormality check
⟨0|1⟩=0.00, ⟨0|0⟩=1.00

Bonus: this is why "measurement basis" is a real choice

Measurement & Stern-Gerlach let you switch the magnet between Z and X — that was switching which orthonormal basis you were reading the state's coordinates in. There was never a "true" basis being hidden from you; both are equally valid coordinate systems for the exact same physical state.
