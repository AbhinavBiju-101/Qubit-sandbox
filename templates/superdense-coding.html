MODULE 2 · QUANTUM ALGORITHMS

Teleportation's mirror image.

Teleportation sends 1 qubit's state using entanglement + 2 classical bits. Superdense coding runs it backwards: send 2 classical bits using entanglement + 1 qubit.

One qubit, four possible messages

Alice and Bob share a Bell pair, made in advance. Alice wants to send Bob one of four 2-bit messages — 00, 01, 10, or 11. She applies one of four gates to her half only (I, X, Z, or XZ), then physically sends that one qubit to Bob. Bob, now holding both qubits, applies CNOT then H and measures both — recovering Alice's exact 2 bits, every time, no errors.

A single unentangled qubit can only ever carry 1 bit reliably. Pre-shared entanglement is what lets one qubit's physical trip carry two classical bits instead — the entanglement was the "prepayment" that makes the extra capacity possible.

Same four gates you already know from Single Qubit — I, X, Z, and X-then-Z — now doing the job of a 2-bit label instead of a Bloch rotation.

3

Bonus: not faster-than-light either

Bob can't decode anything until Alice's physical qubit actually reaches him — the entanglement was shared in advance, but the 2 bits of information only exist for Bob once both qubits are back in the same place. Superdense coding compresses what has to travel, not the universe's speed limit.

4

It isn't free bandwidth

The "2 bits from 1 qubit" headline hides a real cost: the Bell pair had to be created and shared in advance, which itself took a full qubit's worth of quantum communication (plus keeping it coherent until Alice was ready to use it — Decoherence, in Module 5, is exactly the enemy here). Count that setup qubit and the total quantum communication is 2 qubits for 2 bits — no better than sending them classically.

The actual advantage shows up when the entanglement can be distributed ahead of time, during idle capacity, and then spent later exactly when a burst of classical communication is needed — pre-shared entanglement as a stockpiled resource, not a per-message cost. That timing-shift, not a raw bit-count trick, is superdense coding's real practical angle in proposed quantum-network protocols.
