MODULE 4 · WHERE QUANTUM COMES FROM

The wavefunction doesn't stop at the wall.

Classically, a particle without enough energy to clear a barrier just bounces off. Quantum mechanically, its wavefunction leaks through the wall — and sometimes the particle is on the other side.

A wavefunction that decays instead of stopping

At a barrier taller than the particle's energy, classical mechanics says: reflect, 100% of the time. Quantum mechanics says the wavefunction doesn't have to hit zero at the wall — instead it decays exponentially inside the barrier. If the barrier is thin enough that the wavefunction hasn't decayed all the way to zero by the far edge, there's a genuine nonzero amplitude to be found beyond it.

The transmission probability falls off as T ≈ e^{-2κL}, where L is the barrier's width and κ grows with how much the barrier exceeds the particle's energy. Thin and low barriers tunnel easily; thick and tall ones effectively don't — but "effectively don't" is never quite "can't."

You've already met this mechanism without the name: Hardware Lab's Josephson junction is a Cooper pair tunneling straight through an insulating layer a few nanometers thick. Same formula, same physics — the barrier is just an oxide layer instead of a textbook rectangle.

Bonus: this is your qubit's hardware

Every superconducting qubit you've simulated since Physical Qubit relies on exactly this effect: Cooper pairs of electrons tunneling through the thin insulating layer of a Josephson junction. Make that layer too thick and the qubit simply stops working — which is precisely the barrier-width slider above, at chip-fabrication scale.
