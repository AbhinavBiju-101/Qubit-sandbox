Two moves: mark, then amplify

Start every one of the 4 basis states with equal amplitude (25% each) — no idea which one is marked. One Grover iteration is two steps: the oracle flips the sign of the marked item's amplitude (nothing else changes — probabilities are still 25% each, since sign doesn't affect |amplitude|² on its own), then the diffusion operator reflects every amplitude about their average. Because the marked item's amplitude is now negative, that reflection pushes it up sharply while pulling the others down. Step 2 runs the actual numbers.

For exactly 4 items and 1 marked one, the arithmetic works out perfectly: a single iteration takes the marked item from 25% to 100%. That precise number is why 4 items is the cleanest possible demonstration — most sizes need several iterations and never reach exactly 100%.

Grover's real payoff is at scale: for N items, classical search needs about N/2 tries on average, Grover needs about √N — a quadratic speedup that gets more dramatic the bigger N gets, even though this demo's N=4 makes it look almost too easy.
2

The exact arithmetic behind 25% → 100%

Worth checking Step 1's headline number by hand — it isn't a coincidence specific to this app, it falls straight out of "reflect about the mean." Start with all four amplitudes equal: 1/2 each (since (1/2)² = 25%, matching Born's rule from Lesson 0). Say item |11⟩ is marked. The oracle flips only its sign:

AFTER THE ORACLE
|00⟩: +1/2    |01⟩: +1/2    |10⟩: +1/2    |11⟩: −1/2

The mean of these four numbers is (1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 − 1/2) / 4 = (1) / 4 = 1/4. The diffusion operator replaces every amplitude a with 2·mean − a — literally reflecting each point to the opposite side of the average, the same "reflect a point about a line" operation as flipping a number across the midpoint of a number line:

ItemBefore2 × mean − beforeAfterProbability
|00⟩+1/22(1/4) − 1/200%
|01⟩+1/22(1/4) − 1/200%
|10⟩+1/22(1/4) − 1/200%
|11⟩−1/22(1/4) − (−1/2)1100%

Every unmarked item lands on exactly 0, and the marked item lands on exactly 1 — not "amplified a lot," but every last drop of probability concentrated in one place. Notice why the marked item gets pushed so much further than the others get pulled down: it was the one outlier dragging the mean itself downward (from 1/2 to 1/4), so reflecting it about that already-lowered mean sends it unusually far in the positive direction, while the unmarked items — much closer to the mean to begin with — only get nudged a little. One sign flip, hiding in a single term, was enough to visibly skew the entire group's average and therefore everyone's reflection.

Bonus: why phase, not probability, does the work

The oracle step alone changes nothing measurable — all four bars are still 25% right after it, because flipping a sign doesn't change |amplitude|². It's only the diffusion step, acting on that hidden sign difference, that turns invisible phase information into a visible probability swing — Step 2's arithmetic is the receipt for exactly how much swing one sign flip buys. This is the same "phase you can't see directly, but isn't nothing" idea from Single Qubit, Step 4 and Deutsch-Jozsa's phase kickback, doing real algorithmic work a third time.

5

The gap that actually matters: large N

For N items with 1 marked, Grover needs about ⌈(π/4)√N⌉ iterations — each one an oracle-plus-diffusion pair, just like the single round you ran and hand-verified in Step 2. Classically, checking items one at a time takes N/2 tries on average. Pick a database size and compare the two directly.

—Classical: average tries (N/2)
—Grover: iterations (≈π/4·√N)

At N=4, a single iteration reaches exactly 100% because (π/4)√4 ≈ 1.57 rounds to a suspiciously clean 1 — the worked example in Step 2 is really the special case where one round happens to be exactly enough. At larger N, each additional iteration nudges the marked item's probability up a bit more without overshooting, following the same "reflect about the mean" arithmetic repeated over and over, and it takes on the order of √N rounds to climb close to 100%. This quadratic speedup is also provably the best possible for unstructured search — unlike Shor's algorithm's exponential speedup, no future cleverer quantum algorithm will ever beat √N here. That limit was proven, not just unachieved yet.
