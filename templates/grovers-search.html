Two moves: mark, then amplify

Start every one of the 4 basis states with equal amplitude (25% each) — no idea which one is marked. One Grover iteration is two steps: the oracle flips the sign of the marked item's amplitude (nothing else changes — probabilities are still 25% each, since sign doesn't affect |amplitude|² on its own), then the diffusion operator reflects every amplitude about their average. Because the marked item's amplitude is now negative, that reflection pushes it up sharply while pulling the others down.

For exactly 4 items and 1 marked one, the arithmetic works out perfectly: a single iteration takes the marked item from 25% to 100%. That precise number is why 4 items is the cleanest possible demonstration — most sizes need several iterations and never reach exactly 100%.

Grover's real payoff is at scale: for N items, classical search needs about N/2 tries on average, Grover needs about √N — a quadratic speedup that gets more dramatic the bigger N gets, even though this demo's N=4 makes it look almost too easy.

Bonus: why phase, not probability, does the work

The oracle step alone changes nothing measurable — all four bars are still 25% right after it, because flipping a sign doesn't change |amplitude|². It's only the diffusion step, acting on that hidden sign difference, that turns invisible phase information into a visible probability swing. This is the same "phase you can't see directly, but isn't nothing" idea from Complex Numbers, doing real algorithmic work.

4

The gap that actually matters: large N

For N items with 1 marked, Grover needs about ⌈(π/4)√N⌉ iterations — each one an oracle-plus-diffusion pair, just like the single round you ran. Classically, checking items one at a time takes N/2 tries on average. Pick a database size and compare the two directly.
