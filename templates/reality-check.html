PROBLEM 5 · DESIGN THE QUANTUM COMPUTER

From 10 qubits to 100 — what actually gets harder.

The physics of one qubit and the physics of a hundred are the same. The engineering isn't. Here's where today's real machines stand, and the specific bottleneck that shows up first when you scale.

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

How the pieces talk to each other

Classical computer → control system: a conventional server compiles your circuit (the same H/X/RY gates from the Single Qubit Lab) into a schedule of microwave pulses, and sends that schedule to room-temperature control electronics.

Control → qubits: those pulses travel down coaxial lines into the dilution refrigerator, getting attenuated and filtered at each temperature stage, until they reach the chip and physically drive each qubit's rotation.

Qubits → readout → classical computer: resonator signals travel back up through cryogenic amplifiers to room-temperature digitizers, which hand a stream of classical 0s and 1s back to the same server that compiled the circuit — closing the loop.

Why cooling is required: superconductivity itself disappears above a critical temperature, and even below it, thermal energy comparable to the qubit's transition energy will randomly flip or dephase it. Cooling to ~10–15 mK keeps both problems suppressed.

THE BOTTLENECK

Wiring, not physics

Each physical qubit typically needs its own control line (sometimes more, for readout and tuning) running from room temperature down into the dilution refrigerator. At 10 qubits, that's a manageable bundle of coax cables. At 100, it's a real fan-out problem — and the fridge's limited cooling power at its coldest stage means you can't just keep adding wires.

The industry's answer is cryo-CMOS control — putting some control electronics physically inside the fridge, closer to the chip, so a single cable can carry instructions for many qubits instead of one wire per qubit. It trades wiring complexity for a new problem: those control chips now have to work reliably at temperatures conventional electronics were never designed for.

BONUS · ROOM-TEMPERATURE QUANTUM COMPUTING

Does it have to be this cold?

Not always — a few approaches don't need a dilution refrigerator at all, though each pays for it elsewhere:

NV centers in diamond
A nitrogen atom next to a vacancy in diamond's crystal lattice traps an electron whose spin can encode a qubit and be read out optically, at room temperature. Diamond's rigid lattice naturally isolates the spin from thermal noise. Trade-off: individual qubits are easy, but linking many together into one coherent system is still an open problem.
Photonic qubits
Information is carried by photons, which don't interact with heat the way charge-based circuits do, so they can travel through room-temperature waveguides and fiber without decohering the way a transmon would. Trade-off: making photons interact with each other for two-qubit gates is inherently hard, since photons mostly ignore one another.
Topological qubits
A more speculative approach: encode information in the global topology of a system (e.g. theorized Majorana quasiparticles) rather than a fragile local state, so local thermal noise can't easily corrupt it. Still largely experimental — the underlying quasiparticles have been difficult to conclusively demonstrate.

None of these are commercially practical replacements for superconducting qubits yet — but the point of the bonus question isn't a working design, it's showing you understand why cold matters, well enough to reason about how to route around it.
