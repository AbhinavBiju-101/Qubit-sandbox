2

Why qubit count alone is a misleading headline number

The Physical Qubit lesson's readout fidelity and the Hardware Lab lesson's noise sources both show up here in a very concrete way: every single gate in a circuit has some small chance of a small error, and those chances don't add up gently — they compound multiplicatively, the same way "99% reliable" parts wired in series produce a much-less-than-99%-reliable whole system. If one gate succeeds with probability f, then a circuit of N such gates succeeds with probability roughly fᴺ — and exponentials shrink fast.

60.6% chance the whole circuit runs with zero errors

At 99.5% per-gate fidelity — roughly where today's best 2-qubit gates sit — a circuit of just 100 gates already only succeeds cleanly about 61% of the time. A useful algorithm might need thousands of gates; at 1,000 gates, that same 99.5% fidelity gives well under 1% clean-run probability.

This is exactly why Google Willow's "below-threshold" result in Step 1's table is such a big deal, and worth explaining rather than just citing: quantum error correction is a way to spread one logical qubit's information across many physical qubits so that errors on individual physical qubits can be detected and corrected without ever directly measuring (and thus collapsing) the protected information. "Below threshold" means adding more physical qubits to the error-correcting code made the logical error rate go down instead of up — the whole approach only pays off once physical qubits are reliable enough to cross that line. This is why raw qubit count and genuinely useful quantum computation are two different milestones, and why a system with fewer, better qubits can be more useful than one with more, noisier ones.

3

How the pieces talk to each other

SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Classical computer → control system: a conventional server compiles your circuit into a schedule of microwave pulses — literally the pulse-area-and-phase recipe from the Physical Qubit lesson's control step — and sends that schedule to room-temperature control electronics.

Control → qubits: those pulses travel down coaxial lines into the dilution refrigerator, getting attenuated and filtered at each of several temperature stages on the way down, until they reach the chip at its coldest point (~10–15 mK, from Physical Qubit's Step 6).

Qubits → readout → classical computer: resonator signals travel back up through cryogenic amplifiers to room-temperature digitizers, closing the loop — this is the dispersive-readout chain from Physical Qubit's Step 5, drawn end to end.

Why cooling is required: superconductivity disappears above a critical temperature, and even below it, thermal energy comparable to the qubit's transition energy will randomly flip or dephase it.

Every arrow in this diagram is a place a gate error or readout error (Step 2) can sneak in — filters and attenuators distort pulses slightly, amplifiers add noise to the return signal. "One qubit chip" in Step 1's table is really shorthand for this entire loop working reliably, thousands or millions of times per experiment.
4

The bottleneck: wiring, not physics

Each physical qubit typically needs its own control line running from room temperature down into the dilution refrigerator. At 10 qubits, that's a manageable bundle. At 100, it's a real fan-out problem — and the fridge's limited cooling power at its coldest stage means you can't just keep adding wires. This connects directly to the Hardware Lab lesson's "why is the chip so big" surprise: it isn't just the qubits themselves that don't shrink easily, it's everything wired to them.

10 qubits
~10–20 lines
100 qubits
hundreds of lines

The industry's answer is cryo-CMOS control — putting some control electronics physically inside the fridge, closer to the chip, so a single cable can carry instructions for many qubits instead of one wire per qubit. It trades wiring complexity for a new problem: those control chips now have to work reliably at temperatures conventional electronics were never designed for, and any heat they generate has to be removed by the same limited-cooling-power fridge stage.

5

Bonus: does it have to be this cold?

Not always — a few approaches don't need a dilution refrigerator at all, though each pays for it elsewhere:

NV centers in diamond
A nitrogen atom next to a vacancy in diamond's crystal lattice traps an electron whose spin can encode a qubit and be read out optically, at room temperature. Trade-off: linking many together into one coherent system is still an open problem.
Photonic qubits
Photons don't interact with heat the way charge-based circuits do, so they travel through room-temperature waveguides without decohering. Trade-off: making photons interact with each other for two-qubit gates is inherently hard.
Topological qubits
A more speculative approach: encode information in global topology (theorized Majorana quasiparticles) so local thermal noise can't easily corrupt it. Still largely experimental.

None of these are commercially practical replacements for superconducting qubits yet — the point of the bonus question is showing you understand why cold matters, well enough to reason about how to route around it.

Where Module 1 leaves you

Worth pulling the whole module into one line before moving on: a qubit's state is two numbers that have to be squared to become probabilities and can cancel each other out before they do (Lesson 0); every gate is a length-preserving rotation of that state, and phase is real even when a direct measurement can't see it (Lesson 1); combining qubits multiplies the state space instead of adding it, and some joint states can't be pulled back apart into separate descriptions at all (Lesson 2); all of that runs on real superconducting circuits that are simultaneously more fragile and, per-qubit, far larger than any transistor (Lesson 3 and Lesson 4); and today's machines are bottlenecked less by exotic physics than by wiring, cooling, and compounding gate error (this lesson).
