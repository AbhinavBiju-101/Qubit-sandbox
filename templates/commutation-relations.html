MODULE 3 · THE MATH UNDERNEATH

Certain about one means uncertain about the other.

Matrices as Gates showed H and Z landing in different places depending on order. This lesson turns that into the actual physical statement: some pairs of observables can never both be definite at once. Five steps, ending with the whole module tied together.

1

[A, B] = AB − BA

The commutator of two operators, [A, B] = AB − BA, measures exactly how much order matters. If [A, B] = 0, applying them in either order gives the same result — they commute. If not, order matters, and there's a real physical consequence: non-commuting observables can't both have a definite value on the same state at the same time.

For the Pauli matrices, [X, Z] = XZ − ZX = −2iY — nonzero, computed directly from matrices you already know. That single nonzero commutator is the algebraic root of every "measuring one basis disturbs the other" result you've already seen experimentally.

This is the qubit-sized version of Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. The famous ΔxΔp ≥ ħ/2 comes from position and momentum's commutator being nonzero — same mechanism, continuous variables instead of a two-level system.

3

Bonus: [X, Z], computed out

XZ = [[0,-1],[1,0]], and ZX = [[0,1],[-1,0]] — subtract them and you get [[0,-2],[2,0]] = -2iY, exactly as claimed in Step 1. Not asserted, just ordinary 2×2 matrix multiplication, the same operation Matrices as Gates walked through by hand.

4

From "nonzero commutator" to an actual number

The qualitative statement from Step 1 — nonzero commutator means can't both be definite — has a precise quantitative version, the Robertson uncertainty relation: ΔA · ΔB ≥ |⟨[A,B]⟩| / 2, where ΔA and ΔB are each observable's spread of possible outcomes on the state. When [A,B]=0, the right side is zero and there's no obstruction — both can be simultaneously definite, exactly like two commuting gates in Matrices as Gates landing in the same place regardless of order.

Plug in Z and X on the state Step 2 checked: |0⟩ has zero spread in Z (100% certain) and maximum spread in X (50/50), which is exactly what "certain in one basis forces maximal uncertainty in a non-commuting one" looks like as actual numbers, not just a slogan.

5

The whole formal toolkit, in one paragraph

Worth pulling ten lessons into one line before moving on. States are kets, magnitude-squared inner products with themselves normalized to 1 (Complex Numbers, Bra-Ket). Gates are unitary matrices, the exact condition that guarantees they preserve that normalization (Matrices as Gates). Measurement forces a collapse onto one of an observable's eigenvectors, returning its eigenvalue, and different observables can disagree about which basis is "definite" (Measurement, Eigenvalues). Time evolution between measurements is itself just another consequence of one equation, iħ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ (Schrödinger Equation). Any orthonormal set of vectors works as a coordinate system for the same physical state (Basis Vectors), combining systems multiplies dimensions rather than adding them (Tensor Products), and every matrix in the whole toolkit falls into one of exactly three roles depending on one algebraic property (Operator Types). This lesson's non-commuting observables are the reason no basis is ever "the" right one to measure in — incompatibility, not mystery, is what's behind every "measuring X disturbed Z" result since Lesson 0.

None of this changes anything you already believed from Modules 1 and 2 — it's the same physics, the same interference, the same entanglement, now with notation precise enough to prove things instead of just demonstrate them. From here, Module 4 asks where all of this actually came from historically, Module 5 covers what happens when the idealized math meets real, noisy hardware, and Module 6 puts the whole toolkit to work in cryptography.
