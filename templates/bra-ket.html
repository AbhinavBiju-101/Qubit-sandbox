Bra-ket is a label, not new math.

You've been writing states as (α, β) since Single Qubit. Bra-ket notation is just a naming convention for the exact same column vector — this lesson translates, it doesn't introduce. Five steps, including why the bra needs a conjugate and not just a sideways vector.

1

Ket, bra, and the thing between them

A ket |ψ⟩ is just a column vector — for one qubit, that's the pair (α, β) you already know. A bra ⟨ψ| is the same vector laid on its side and complex-conjugated (flip the sign of every imaginary part): ⟨ψ| = (α*, β*). Step 2 explains why the conjugate specifically is non-negotiable.

Put a bra in front of a ket and you get their inner product, a single complex number: ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = φ0*ψ0 + φ1*ψ1. You've already used the special case of this constantly — ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = |α|² + |β|² = 1 is exactly the normalization rule from QM Basics.

General rule, one case you already knew: ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ is a state's inner product with itself, which is why normalizing a state and computing ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1 are the same sentence.
2

Why the conjugate — not just a sideways vector

Worth asking directly: why does turning a ket into a bra involve conjugating, rather than just laying the same numbers on their side? Try the plain-transpose version without conjugating and see what breaks. For a state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, the un-conjugated "inner product with itself" would be α² + β² — and for complex α, β, that number generally isn't even real, let alone a valid probability between 0 and 1. Try it with α = i/√2, β = 1/√2 (a perfectly legal, normalized state): α² + β² = (i/√2)² + (1/√2)² = −1/2 + 1/2 = 0 — a state with 100% real probability of existing would report zero "length." Clearly not what a normalization check should say.

Conjugating fixes this by construction, using exactly the fact from Complex Numbers, Step 1: for any complex number z, z* · z = |z|² — always real, always non-negative, regardless of z's phase. So ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = α*α + β*β = |α|² + |β|² is guaranteed to be a real, non-negative number no matter what phases α and β carry — exactly what's required for it to mean "total probability." The conjugate in the bra isn't a notational quirk; it's the one modification that makes "inner product with yourself" land back in the world of real, meaningful numbers instead of wandering off into the complex plane.

⟨φ|ψ⟩ — the number that predicts measurement

Pick a reference state |φ⟩ below. We'll compute ⟨φ|ψ⟩ against the |ψ⟩ you built in Step 3, and — this is the payoff — |⟨φ|ψ⟩|² is exactly the probability of getting outcome φ if you measured in a basis that includes it. Born's rule was always this formula in disguise. Worked example to check the sandbox against: with |ψ⟩ = |1⟩ (click X first) and |φ⟩ = |+⟩, by hand: ⟨+|1⟩ = (1/√2)(0) + (1/√2)(1) = 1/√2, so |⟨+|1⟩|² = 1/2 = 50% — matching the familiar fact that |1⟩ measured in the +/− basis (Lesson 0, Step 6) is a coin flip.

5

Flip it around: outer products build operators

A bra times a ket, ⟨φ|ψ⟩, is a number. A ket times a bra, |φ⟩⟨ψ| — the outer product — is a matrix instead. |0⟩⟨0| is exactly the projector from Operator Types: it's the matrix [[1,0],[0,0]], built from nothing but a ket and a bra multiplied the other way around.

This generalizes further than one projector: any operator can be written as a sum of outer products. Z, for instance, is exactly |0⟩⟨0| − |1⟩⟨1| — its two eigenvectors, each weighted by its own eigenvalue. That's not a special trick for Z; it's the general recipe (the "spectral decomposition") for rebuilding any Hermitian operator once you know its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, which is exactly what Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors covers next.
