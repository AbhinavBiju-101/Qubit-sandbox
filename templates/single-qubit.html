What is a qubit, actually?

A classical bit is 0 or 1 — nothing in between. A qubit's state is a pair of complex numbers (α, β) called amplitudes, one for |0⟩ and one for |1⟩, with the rule |α|² + |β|² = 1. (New to this? Lesson 0 derives where that rule comes from, with real numbers only — worth a detour if this paragraph moved fast.)

You never see α and β directly. What you get on measurement is 0 with probability |α|², or 1 with probability |β|² — that's Born's rule, and it's the only place randomness enters quantum mechanics.

A qubit starting at |0⟩ has α = 1, β = 0 — measuring it always gives 0. Nothing quantum has happened yet. The interesting part starts in Step 2, once a gate moves that pair of numbers somewhere else.

Apply gates and watch the state move

The Bloch sphere is a way to draw a qubit's state as a point on a sphere: the north pole is |0⟩, the south pole is |1⟩, and everywhere else is a superposition. A gate is just a rotation of that point.

Bonus: skew the coin to 75% / 25%

A Hadamard always gives 50/50 because it rotates exactly to the equator. To land anywhere else, rotate by a smaller angle instead — that's what RY(θ) does. For P(0) ≈ 75%, solve cos²(θ/2) = 0.75, which gives θ ≈ 60°.
