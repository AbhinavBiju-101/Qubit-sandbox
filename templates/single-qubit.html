What is a qubit, actually?

A classical bit is 0 or 1 — nothing in between. A qubit's state is a pair of complex numbers (α, β) called amplitudes, one for |0⟩ and one for |1⟩, with the rule |α|² + |β|² = 1. You never see α and β directly. What you get on measurement is 0 with probability |α|², or 1 with probability |β|² — that's Born's rule, and it's the only place randomness enters quantum mechanics.

Crucially, α and β aren't a stand-in for a hidden classical fact you just haven't looked at yet — they're a complete description in their own right. A qubit isn't a coin spinning in the air that's "really" going to land on a definite face; the superposition is the whole story until the moment you measure. Keep that distinction in mind through this lesson: whenever the Bloch sphere below shows a point between the poles, that's not "50% chance it's secretly at the north pole" — it's a single, definite, in-between state, and Step 4 shows a way to actually detect the difference.

A qubit starting at |0⟩ has α = 1, β = 0 — measuring it always gives 0. Nothing quantum has happened yet. The interesting part starts once a gate moves that pair of numbers somewhere else — which raises an obvious question answered in Step 2: what's even allowed to move it, and where can it go?
2

Why every gate has to be a rotation

A gate is just an operation you apply to a qubit's state — mechanically, a 2×2 grid of (complex) numbers that multiplies the (α, β) pair to produce a new (α, β) pair. But not any 2×2 grid is a legal gate. Remember from Lesson 0 that α² + β² has to equal 1, always, no exceptions — a gate that didn't preserve that would take a perfectly valid state and turn it into an invalid one, "creating" or "destroying" probability out of nowhere. So every legal gate is restricted to the one family of operations that's guaranteed to keep a normalized vector normalized: rotations. (The formal name for this length-preserving property is unitary — you'll meet the matrix condition that defines it precisely in the Module 3 Matrices as Gates lesson; for now, "rotation" is the right mental picture and the right word for it.)

This is exactly why the Bloch sphere — a way to draw a qubit's state as a single point on the surface of a sphere, north pole = |0⟩, south pole = |1⟩, everywhere else a superposition — is the natural picture for a qubit and not just a nice illustration. A rotation of a sphere always keeps every point on the sphere's surface; it can never fling a point off into the middle or out past the edge. "Every gate is a rotation" and "every state stays on the sphere" are the same fact, said two ways.

One useful consequence to predict before you even touch the sandbox: rotations are reversible — for every gate there's exactly one gate that undoes it (spin the sphere back). That means, on its own, no sequence of gates can ever erase information about a state or make a measurement's outcome any more certain than the geometry allows. All the "processing power" of a quantum computer has to come from where you rotate to and how different paths interfere (Lesson 0, Step 5) — never from gates secretly being lossy.

Stop and think: what happens if you reset, then click H twice in a row?
You land back exactly on |0⟩. H really does rotate 45° in a certain sense — do it twice and you've gone "all the way," which for this particular rotation means returning home. Nothing was measured in between, so this isn't luck or a coincidence of probabilities — the two H's compose into a rotation that happens to be the identity. That's a hint of interference already: whatever amplitude combination the first H put on the |1⟩ slot has to exactly cancel back out for the second H to return you to pure |0⟩. Step 4 pulls that cancellation apart in slow motion.
4

Same probabilities, different qubit — the phase you can't see (until you can)

Here's a state Born's rule alone can't tell apart from |+⟩: apply Z to |+⟩ and you land on the point called |−⟩, on the opposite side of the equator. Both |+⟩ and |−⟩ measure 50/50 in the standard 0/1 basis — if all you ever did was measure right away, you could never tell them apart. Does that mean they're really "the same state" as far as anything is concerned? Use the Bloch sphere from Step 3 to find out — it's the same qubit object, so this continues wherever you left it:

Try this sequence on the Step 3 sandbox above: click Reset, then H, then Z, then H again — and watch the state readout after each click.

After H, you're at |+⟩ (50/50). After Z, you're at |−⟩ — still 50/50, indistinguishable by direct measurement. But after the second H, you land exactly on |1⟩ — a certain outcome, and a different certain outcome than plain H-then-H-with-no-Z-in-between would give you (which was |0⟩, from Step 3's experiment). The extra Z in the middle was invisible to a direct measurement, yet it completely changed what the next gate produced. That invisible-until-it-isn't property is called phase, and this experiment is proof it's physically real, not decoration — it's the same interference mechanism from Lesson 0's Step 5, now happening inside an actual gate sequence instead of a toy arrow diagram.

DIAGRAM · THE EQUATOR, VIEWED FROM ABOVE

|+⟩ and |−⟩ sit on opposite sides of the same equator — same "latitude" (so the same 0/1 split), different "longitude" (phase). A direct 0/1 measurement only ever reads latitude. Longitude only shows up once you rotate before measuring, exactly as the H–Z–H experiment just did.

This is the mechanism, not a metaphor for it: every quantum algorithm that beats a classical computer works by steering the wrong answers' amplitudes to land on opposite phases so a later gate cancels them out, while the right answer's amplitude reinforces. You've now personally produced that cancellation once (H–H) and demonstrated that phase survives even when a measurement can't see it directly (H–Z–H). File this experiment away — Module 2's Deutsch-Jozsa and Grover's Search lessons are this exact trick scaled up to real problems.

5

Measure it — 1,000 shots

Measuring does two things at once, and it's worth separating them clearly: it produces one random outcome (0 or 1, weighted by Born's rule), and it destroys the superposition — the qubit's state genuinely becomes exactly |0⟩ or exactly |1⟩ afterward, not just "as far as you can tell." This is called collapse, and unlike the gates in Steps 2–4, it is not reversible; there's no gate that takes a collapsed |0⟩ back to whatever superposition it used to be, because the information about what that superposition was is simply gone. (It's also, not coincidentally, the reason you can't peek at a qubit "gently" to check its amplitudes without disturbing it — a fact with its own name, the no-cloning theorem, that the Physical Qubit and Two Qubits lessons lean on.)

Each shot below re-prepares the same state and measures fresh — the qubit doesn't remember previous shots, so re-running gives slightly different counts. That's expected statistical noise, not a bug: with N shots at true probability p, the count you see typically wanders by about √(p(1−p)/N) from the true value — for a 50/50 state at N=1000 that's roughly ±1.6 percentage points, which is exactly the size of wobble you should expect to see below. 6

Bonus: skew the coin to 75% / 25%

A Hadamard always gives 50/50 because it rotates exactly to the equator. To land anywhere else, rotate by a smaller angle instead — that's what RY(θ) does: a partial rotation toward the equator, stopping wherever you tell it to. This is precisely the quarter-circle slider from Lesson 0, Step 3, now literally wrapped around a sphere — dragging θ from 0° to 180° sweeps the same α = cos(θ/2), β = sin(θ/2) path you dragged there by hand, just parameterized by angle instead of by α directly.

Work the target angle out algebraically before touching the slider: you want P(0) = cos²(θ/2) = 0.75, so cos(θ/2) = √0.75 ≈ 0.866, so θ/2 = arccos(0.866) ≈ 30°, so θ ≈ 60°. Drag to 60° and confirm the readout agrees with the arithmetic.

Notice θ = 180° gives P(0) = cos²(90°) = 0 — a full rotation from pole to pole, i.e. exactly the X gate from Step 3. RY isn't a different kind of gate from H and X; H, X, and every other single-qubit gate you'll meet are all specific angles (and axes) of the same general rotation. RY(θ) is the whole continuous family, and the named gates are just its convenient, frequently-used stops.

How good is our toy simulator, actually?

Everything above runs on this app's own from-scratch complex-matrix simulator. This step sends your exact circuit — whatever gates you've applied in Step 3/4, or the RY(θ) from Step 6 — to a Flask endpoint that runs it on real IBM Qiskit (qiskit-aer's AerSimulator), then runs 1,000 shots on both so you can compare the statistical noise directly.

Worth being precise about what this step can and can't validate. Both simulators compute the exact same Born-rule probabilities from the exact same underlying math — this comparison checks that your sandbox's arithmetic agrees with a production-grade library's arithmetic, not that either one behaves like physical hardware. A real chip's shots also carry hardware noise — gate errors, decoherence, readout errors — on top of the shot noise both simulators show here; that's a different, additional effect the Physical Qubit and Hardware Lab lessons deal with directly, and it's why the gap you're about to compare here should track shot noise closely, while a comparison against real hardware would not.
