1

Constant, or balanced?

You're handed a hidden function f(x) that takes one bit and returns one bit, promised to be either constant (same output for every input) or balanced (outputs 0 for half the inputs, 1 for the other half). For a single-bit input there are only two inputs, so classically you might get lucky on 1 query — but in the worst case you need both queries to be sure.

The quantum trick: put the input in superposition, run the oracle once, and interference cancels out everything except the one bit of information you actually need — constant or balanced — in a single query, guaranteed, every time. Step 2 makes that "cancels out" claim precise instead of just asserting it.

The oracle is wired as |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩ — the CNOT and X gates you already know from Two Qubits, just relabeled as "the unknown function" for this lesson.
2

The algebra: phase kickback and interference

The sandbox's circuit starts qubit y at |1⟩, not |0⟩ — that's not an arbitrary choice, it's the entire trick, and it's worth walking through once by hand. After H on both qubits: x is (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, and y is (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 — the state this app calls |−⟩ back in Single Qubit, Step 4's phase experiment.

Now watch what the oracle |x⟩|y⟩ → |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩ does to y = |−⟩ specifically. If f(x) = 0, y is untouched: still |−⟩. If f(x) = 1, XOR-ing 1 into y flips it — but flipping |−⟩ (swap the |0⟩ and |1⟩ terms) gives (|1⟩−|0⟩)/√2 = −|−⟩: the same state, just with an overall minus sign out front. Either way, y itself comes back completely unchanged in every respect a measurement could ever detect — but that minus sign, when f(x)=1, doesn't just vanish. Because x and y are entangled through the oracle, that sign attaches itself to the x branch that produced it. This effect — a target qubit that looks untouched, secretly handing its phase to the control — is called phase kickback, and it's one of the most reused tricks in every quantum algorithm from here on, Grover's Search included.

RESULT: x PICKS UP A SIGN PER BRANCH
x becomes: [ (−1)f(0)|0⟩ + (−1)f(1)|1⟩ ] / √2

Now the final H on x is where Step 1's "interference cancels everything except the answer" stops being a slogan. There are only two cases:

Case 1: f is constant (f(0) = f(1))
Both signs are the same — either both + or both −. Factor out the shared sign (a global phase, which Single Qubit's Step 4 already established is invisible to any measurement) and x is left as plain (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. Applying H to that — literally the double-H experiment from Single Qubit, Step 3, run once instead of twice — lands exactly back on |0⟩. Measuring x always gives 0.
Case 2: f is balanced (f(0) ≠ f(1))
The signs differ — one +, one −. Now x is (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 = |−⟩. Applying H to |−⟩ lands exactly on |1⟩ (the reverse of the H that made |−⟩ from |1⟩ in the first place). Measuring x always gives 1.

That's the whole mechanism: the relative sign between f(0) and f(1) — invisible to any single measurement on its own, exactly the "phase you can't see until you can" idea from Single Qubit, Step 4 — is exactly what determines whether the final H constructively reinforces toward |0⟩ or destructively flips everything to |1⟩. Nothing about individual outcomes was ever measured or needed; the single global fact "same sign or different sign" is all Step 1's one query was ever really asking for, and interference is precisely the mechanism that turns that fact into a clean, deterministic answer.

4

Bonus: this scales

With n input bits, a classical worst case needs 2ⁿ⁻¹ + 1 queries to be certain — exponentially many. The quantum algorithm still needs exactly one, regardless of n. Step 2's derivation generalizes cleanly: with n input qubits all starting in superposition and the same |−⟩ trick on the output qubit, the oracle kicks back a sign (−1)^f(x) onto every one of the 2ⁿ branches simultaneously — not one at a time, all at once, because they were all in superposition together — and the final round of Hadamards on every input qubit is what collapses that entire pattern of signs down to a single readable answer. The 1-bit version here is the smallest case where that gap already exists; it just isn't dramatic yet at n=1.

5

Nobody runs this for a real problem — and that's the point

Be honest about what you just built: "is this specific contrived function constant or balanced" isn't a problem anyone actually has. Deutsch-Jozsa's real importance is historical and structural, not practical — published in 1992, it was the first algorithm to give a rigorous, provable case where a quantum computer needs exponentially fewer queries than any possible classical algorithm, for any input size. Before it, "quantum speedup" was a hope; after it, it was a theorem.

Every algorithm since has built on the same trick you just derived in Step 2: superposition, an oracle that kicks back phase instead of flipping a visible bit, then interference to cancel out everything except the one bit of global information you actually wanted. Grover's Search (next lesson) and Shor's Algorithm (Module 6) are both, at their core, more sophisticated versions of exactly this pattern — Grover's especially reuses phase kickback almost verbatim, just repeated and combined with a reflection step instead of a single Hadamard round.
