Finding a hidden period is the whole trick.

RSA encryption's security rests on factoring large numbers being classically hard. Shor's algorithm doesn't factor directly — it finds a repeating period exponentially faster, and period-finding turns out to be all factoring needs.

From "find the period" to "here are the factors"

Pick a number a smaller than N (the number you want to factor) with no common factors. The sequence a¹ mod N, a² mod N, a³ mod N, … always repeats — call that repeat length its period r. Once you know r, a little classical algebra (gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) and gcd(a^(r/2) + 1, N)) hands you N's factors directly, most of the time.

Finding r classically means checking values one by one — for an N with hundreds of digits (real RSA key sizes), that's longer than the age of the universe. Shor's quantum algorithm finds r using a quantum Fourier transform in roughly polynomial time instead — the exponential-vs-polynomial gap is the entire reason a large enough quantum computer would break RSA.

Everything below this box is the easy, classical half of Shor's algorithm — the part any computer can do once r is known. The genuinely quantum half, finding r fast, needs more qubits and more error correction than exists today (see NISQ) to run on numbers anywhere near RSA scale.

Bonus: BB84 vs. Shor's — two different quantum threats

Worth keeping straight: BB84 (previous lesson) is quantum mechanics defending a key exchange. Shor's algorithm is quantum computing attacking classical RSA/factoring-based cryptography. That's not a contradiction — they're solving different problems, which is exactly why "quantum-safe" cryptography research exists: replace factoring-based schemes with ones Shor's algorithm doesn't help against, while quantum key distribution like BB84 sidesteps the whole issue by not relying on factoring in the first place.
