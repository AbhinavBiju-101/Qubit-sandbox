1

What the acronym is actually saying

Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum, coined by physicist John Preskill in 2018. Each word is doing real work: intermediate-scale means enough qubits to be interesting (tens to low thousands) but nowhere near enough for the error-corrected, fault-tolerant machines quantum algorithms were originally designed for. Noisy means those qubits don't have error correction wrapped around them the way Bit-Flip and Phase-Flip Codes just showed — every gate has some real chance of a small error, and those errors compound.

That compounding is the whole story of why NISQ matters: a circuit's overall success probability falls off roughly as (1 − ε)ᴺ, where ε is the per-gate error rate and N is the number of gates. Even a small ε caps how deep (how many sequential gates) a useful circuit can be before noise drowns out the signal — which is exactly why NISQ-era algorithms are designed to be shallow.

Full error correction like Bit-Flip Codes fixes this in principle — but at a steep cost: today's best codes need on the order of 1,000+ noisy physical qubits per 1 clean logical qubit. NISQ is the name for the gap between "we have qubits" and "we have enough spare qubits to error-correct them."

3

So why bother, right now? The variational loop

Step 2's curve looks discouraging, but there's a genuine strategy for living inside it: variational algorithms (VQE and QAOA are the two best-known), which don't try to run one long, deep, precise circuit at all. Instead they run a short loop, repeatedly:

  1. Run a shallow circuit — few enough gates that Step 2's curve is still forgiving — built from a handful of tunable parameters (rotation angles, essentially RY(θ)-style knobs from Single Qubit, Step 6, just many of them at once).
  2. Measure the output and compute one number from it (an "expectation value" — how good this attempt was for the problem at hand).
  3. Hand that one number to an ordinary classical computer, which nudges the parameters in a direction that should improve it — classical optimization, nothing quantum about this step.
  4. Repeat with the new parameters.

The quantum computer never has to get the whole answer in one long, fragile shot; it only ever has to survive a short, noise-tolerant circuit many times over, while a classical computer does the heavy lifting of steering the search. This hybrid division of labor — quantum for the part only a quantum computer can do, classical for everything else — is the dominant practical strategy for extracting value from hardware before full error correction arrives, and it's specifically why NISQ-era research leans so heavily on shallow circuits rather than trying to brute-force through the noise.

4

Benchmarking a real device: quantum volume

Raw qubit count alone is a poor way to compare devices — a 100-qubit chip with weak connectivity and mediocre fidelity can be less useful than a well-connected 27-qubit chip with excellent gates. Quantum volume (introduced by IBM) tries to capture the whole picture in one number: it runs increasingly demanding random circuits (more qubits and more circuit depth together) and reports the largest square circuit — n qubits, n layers deep — the device can run successfully often enough. It bundles qubit count, connectivity, and the exact (1-ε)ᴺ curve from Step 2 into a single comparable figure.

It's not the only metric in use (others weight gate speed or specific application performance differently), and it's specifically not a measure of anything past the NISQ era — once real error correction is running, a "logical qubit count" replaces it as the number that actually matters. For now, though, quantum volume is the closest thing the field has to a single "how good is this chip" score.

5

Idealized math meets real hardware, in one paragraph

Worth pulling three lessons into one line before moving on. Every gate in Modules 1–3 was a clean, length-preserving rotation — real qubits leak information to their environment instead, which shows up as the Bloch vector physically shrinking, a phenomenon that needs a genuinely new mathematical object (the density matrix) to even describe (Why Qubits Decohere). You can fight that leakage without ever peeking at the protected data, by spreading one logical qubit across several physical ones and reading only parity information (Bit-Flip and Phase-Flip Codes). And today's real machines don't have nearly enough spare qubits to run that protection fully — so the field runs short, noise-tolerant circuits instead, with a classical computer steering a quantum one (this lesson).

This is the honest state of the field, not a simplification for teaching purposes — every real device Reality Check named lives exactly here. From here, Module 6 shows one place this NISQ-era hardware is already genuinely useful today, not someday: quantum cryptography, where even a single, noisy, unentangled qubit is enough to build a communication protocol that's provably secure against any eavesdropper, quantum or classical.
