Young's double slit, and the puzzle it left behind

Shine light (or fire electrons) through two narrow, closely-spaced slits at a screen. Waves passing through both slits interfere: crest-meets-crest gives a bright band, crest-meets-trough cancels to dark — a striped fringe pattern, not two simple bands.

The puzzle: this still happens when particles go through one at a time, slow enough that only one particle is ever in flight. Each one lands as a single point — clearly a particle. But over thousands of them, the points build the exact same striped pattern — clearly a wave. Both descriptions are correct; neither is complete alone. That's wave-particle duality.

Every amplitude you've used since Single Qubit already carries this: a state is a wave-like sum of possibilities right up until measurement, at which point it commits to one particle-like outcome. The double slit is that same story, just running in physical space instead of a Bloch sphere.
2

The exact formula — the same arrows from Lesson 0, now in real space

Step 3's simulator secretly draws from the density (1 + cos(phase))/2 at each screen position. Worth deriving that instead of taking it on faith — it's Lesson 0, Step 5's two-arrows-adding picture, run with an actual physical variable instead of an abstract toy example. A particle reaching screen position x can get there via slit 1 or slit 2, and — exactly as Lesson 0 established — quantum mechanics says you add the two amplitudes for those paths, not the two probabilities.

Each path's amplitude has the same size (the slits are identical) but a different phase, because the two paths are slightly different lengths — and a wave's phase advances steadily as it travels, so a longer path arrives phase-shifted relative to a shorter one. Call that phase difference φ. The total amplitude at x is A(1 + e^{iφ}) for some constant A, and Born's rule (probability = amplitude magnitude squared, same rule since Lesson 0) gives:

P(x) ∝ |1 + e^{iφ}|² = 2 + 2cos(φ) = 4·cos²(φ/2)

Where φ=0 (equal path lengths, dead center) the two amplitudes point the same direction and add fully — a bright fringe, 4× a single slit's rate, not 2×, exactly the constructive-interference arithmetic from Lesson 0, Step 5. Where φ=π (paths differ by half a wavelength) the amplitudes point opposite directions and cancel completely — a dark fringe, zero particles landing there, even though each individual slit, covered alone, would happily send particles to that exact spot. The phase φ itself depends on position through simple geometry (φ is proportional to d·x/λ, where d is slit separation), which is exactly why Step 4's sliders for d and λ stretch or squeeze the fringe spacing — they're changing how fast φ sweeps through a full cycle as x moves across the screen.

Toggle the which-path detector on and the fringes flatten to a plain double-bump pattern — Step 2's formula reduces to just adding probabilities instead of amplitudes once you know which slit each particle went through, because forcing a definite answer to "which path?" is a measurement, and Lesson 0, Step 6 already established that a definite answer to one question can make a related question's interference vanish. The detector doesn't jostle the particle into a different physical path here — it destroys the very superposition of "both paths" that the interference formula depended on.

5

Bonus: what if you decide to look after the fact?

A genuinely strange real experiment (delayed-choice, and its more careful cousin the delayed-choice quantum eraser) arranges the which-path detector so the decision to check it — or to erase that information instead — happens after each particle has already passed through the slits, but before it's recorded on the screen. The result: interference appears or vanishes based on that later choice, exactly as if which-path information had or hadn't been available all along.

Worth being precise about what this does and doesn't mean: nothing here sends a signal backward in time or lets you influence the past — you only ever learn which outcome you got by comparing detector and screen records afterward, and no individual measurement result can be predicted or controlled. It's less "retrocausality" and more a sharp reminder that "did the particle take a definite path" was never a well-defined question until an actual measurement settled it.
