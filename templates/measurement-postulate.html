The measurement postulate, stated once

Measuring an observable can only return one of its allowed outcomes, with probability |⟨outcome|ψ⟩|² (Step 3 of Bra-Ket, generalized), and the state collapses to that outcome afterward. Silver atoms in a Stern-Gerlach magnet only ever land in two spots — "spin up" or "spin down" along the magnet's axis — never in between, exactly like your qubit only ever measures 0 or 1.

One more piece worth naming: the expectation value ⟨Z⟩ = p(up) − p(down) is the average result over many atoms, not any single atom's outcome — it's what "50/50" and "75/25" have been summarizing all along.

Original 1922 experiment, exactly this app's engine: an oven sprays silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnetic field. Classically you'd expect a smear (any deflection angle). What Stern and Gerlach actually saw: two sharp spots. That binary outcome is a qubit measurement, discovered two decades before the word "qubit" existed.

4

Everything above assumed a perfect measurement

The projective measurement you just simulated — clean collapse onto an exact eigenvector, with probabilities given exactly by |⟨outcome|ψ⟩|² — is the textbook ideal. Real hardware readout is noisier: a superconducting qubit's measurement might report "0" when the qubit was actually in |1⟩, typically a few percent of the time on current devices. That's called readout error, and it's a separate, additional problem on top of the gate errors NISQ (Module 5) covers.

The general mathematical tool for describing imperfect, noisy, or partial measurements is called a POVM (positive operator-valued measure) — a generalization of the clean projectors from Operator Types that can describe "measure, but with some chance of getting it wrong" directly in the math. This app always simulates the ideal case; real device calibration reports (the kind Reality Check pulls numbers from) are where you'd see the actual readout-error rate for a given piece of hardware.
