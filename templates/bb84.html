Two bases, and the measurement postulate doing security work

For each bit, Alice picks a random value (0 or 1) and a random basis — Z (|0⟩/|1⟩) or X (|+⟩/|−⟩) — and sends a qubit prepared accordingly. Bob measures each incoming qubit in his own randomly chosen basis. When his basis happens to match Alice's, he gets her bit back with certainty; when it doesn't, he gets a random, useless result. Afterward, Alice and Bob publicly compare which bases they used (never the bits) and keep only the positions where they matched — that surviving subset is the "sifted key."

Here's the security: Measurement & Stern-Gerlach already showed you that measuring in the wrong basis genuinely disturbs a state. If Eve intercepts a qubit and measures it (she doesn't know Alice's basis either, so she guesses), roughly half the time she guesses wrong and disturbs it before resending — introducing errors that show up when Alice and Bob compare a sample of their sifted key out loud.

No eavesdropping ⇒ sifted-key error rate ≈ 0%. Eve intercepting-and-resending every qubit ⇒ error rate ≈ 25% on the sifted key. That gap isn't a software patch someone could sneak past — it's the measurement postulate itself, working as a tripwire.

3

Bonus: this secures the key, not the message

BB84 only gets Alice and Bob a shared random secret string with a provable eavesdropping check — the actual message still gets encrypted classically (commonly with that key as a one-time pad, or feeding a standard symmetric cipher). The quantum part's entire job is solving "how do two people agree on a secret over a public channel without anyone else usably copying it" — which, per the no-cloning theorem, Shor's Algorithm's next lesson will connect to why factoring-based classical cryptography is the thing actually at risk from quantum computers, not this.

4

BB84's cousin: security from a Bell inequality instead

BB84 uses single qubits prepared and measured in random bases. A related protocol, E91 (Artur Ekert, 1991), instead distributes entangled Bell pairs — the exact states from Two Qubits — one particle to Alice, one to Bob. Its security proof works differently: Alice and Bob check whether their correlated measurement results violate a Bell inequality (a statistical test that no classical, non-entangled explanation could pass). If the violation is there, genuine entanglement was present and untampered with; if Eve had intercepted and measured the particles, the entanglement — and the violation — would be destroyed.

This gives E91 a property BB84 doesn't have on its own: the security proof doesn't need to trust that Alice and Bob's devices are working correctly, only that the statistics violate the inequality — a idea called device-independent security, an active area of real quantum-cryptography research.
