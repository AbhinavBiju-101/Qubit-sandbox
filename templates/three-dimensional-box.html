1

Three quantum numbers, one energy formula

Confine a particle to a cube instead of a line, and the wavefunction has to satisfy the same "pinned at the walls" condition in all three directions at once, giving it three independent quantum numbers nₓ, n_y, n_z (each 1, 2, 3, …). The energy is just the 1D formula added across all three: E(nₓ,n_y,n_z) ∝ nₓ² + n_y² + n_z².

Because it's a sum of squares, completely different triples of quantum numbers can land on the exact same total — (1,2,2) and (2,1,2) and (2,2,1) all give 1+4+4=9. Each distinct triple is still a physically distinct state (a different standing-wave pattern along each axis) — they just happen to share an energy. That's degeneracy, computed instead of asserted.

A perfectly cubic box gets extra degeneracy specifically from its symmetry — permuting nₓ,n_y,n_z always preserves the sum of squares. Stretch the box into a non-cube and most of that degeneracy breaks, because the three directions no longer contribute equally to the energy.

Bonus: this is where atomic orbital degeneracy comes from

A hydrogen atom's electron shells show exactly this kind of degeneracy — multiple orbitals (different shapes, different quantum numbers) sharing one energy level, purely from the symmetry of the confining potential. The cubic box here is a toy model, but the mechanism generating degenerate levels is the same one running in every atom.

4

From nₓ,n_y,n_z to n,l,m

A real hydrogen atom's confining potential is spherical (the electron's attraction to the nucleus), not a cubic box, so it isn't literally this same calculation — it needs three different quantum numbers, conventionally called n, l, and m, that come from solving the Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates instead of Cartesian ones. But the structural pattern is identical: three-dimensional confinement produces three independent quantum numbers, and different combinations of them can land on the same energy.

That's exactly why a hydrogen atom's n=2 shell contains one 2s orbital and three different 2p orbitals (differing in their m quantum number, or equivalently their orientation) all sharing the same energy — real atomic orbital degeneracy, produced by the identical "symmetric confinement → shared quantum numbers → shared energy" mechanism this cubic box demonstrated, just running on the sphere instead of the cube.

5

The history, in one paragraph

Worth pulling six lessons into one line before moving on. Classical physics predicted infinite energy from a hot object; Planck's fix — energy in discrete packets — is where "quantum" got its name (Blackbody Radiation). Particles turned out to have wavelengths too, via λ=h/p (de Broglie), which the double slit showed was real and not just a formula — interference from single particles, one at a time (Wave-Particle Duality). Confine that wave and only certain standing-wave shapes fit, which is where discrete energy levels come from without any extra hypothesis (Particle in a Box, this lesson). A wave doesn't have to stop dead at a barrier taller than its energy — it decays, and sometimes reappears on the other side (Quantum Tunneling).

None of this was invented to explain qubits — it's the actual, decades-long historical struggle (roughly 1900 to 1930) to explain ordinary matter and light, full of experiments that flatly refused to match classical predictions. The state vectors, superposition, and interference from Modules 1–3 are the same mathematics this module's phenomena forced physicists to invent, just abstracted down to the simplest possible 2-level system. From here, Module 5 asks what happens when this idealized math meets real, imperfect, noisy hardware, and Module 6 puts entanglement and interference to work breaking and building cryptographic systems.
