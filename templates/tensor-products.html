Combining two spaces: dimensions multiply

When two independent quantum systems are described together, their combined state space is the tensor product of the two, written ⊗. Each qubit's space is 2-dimensional; two qubits together live in a 2 × 2 = 4-dimensional space — not 2 + 2 = 4, multiply, which is why n qubits give 2ⁿ dimensions and why classical simulation of quantum computers gets hard so fast.

For two unentangled qubits with amplitudes (α₀, α₁) and (β₀, β₁), the joint 4-dimensional state is built by multiplying every pair: (α₀β₀, α₀β₁, α₁β₀, α₁β₁), in the order |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩.

This is exactly the recipe your very first two-qubit state used — before any CNOT, two independently-prepared qubits are always a tensor product, by construction.

The 2ⁿ wall

Step 1 said dimensions multiply — 2 qubits need 4 amplitudes, 3 need 8, and in general n qubits need 2ⁿ complex numbers to describe a fully general (possibly entangled) state. Pick n below and see how fast that grows.

Past roughly 50 qubits, that number exceeds what every computer on Earth combined could store as ordinary floating-point numbers — which is the actual, concrete reason a classical computer can't just brute-force-simulate a large quantum computer's full state, and why this app's own multi-qubit engine stays deliberately small (2-5 qubits) everywhere it's used.
