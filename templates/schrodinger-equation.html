Two versions of one equation

The time-independent Schrödinger equation finds a system's fixed energy levels — for a qubit, that's just |0⟩ and |1⟩ again, now called energy eigenstates. The time-dependent version says how a state changes moment to moment: each energy eigenstate spins in the complex plane at a rate set by its energy, e^{-iEt/ħ}.

Here's the part that matters for what you'll actually see: if a qubit is sitting purely in |0⟩ or purely in |1⟩, that spinning is an invisible global phase — nothing observable moves. It's only a superposition of the two — different energies spinning at different rates, drifting apart — that shows up as motion on the Bloch sphere. That drift is called precession.

This is the same phase gate from Complex Numbers, just now running continuously instead of by a slider you set once.

Rabi flopping — how a real qubit gets controlled

Drive a qubit with a resonant pulse and, instead of precessing around the pole, it rotates all the way from |0⟩ to |1⟩ and back — a continuous version of the single-click H/X gates from Module 1. 4

Where two-qubit gates actually come from

Everything above evolved one qubit under its own Hamiltonian. A CNOT or CZ gate between two qubits comes from exactly the same equation, just with a Hamiltonian that couples them — a term describing how each qubit's energy depends on the other's state. Physically, that coupling is usually a tunable interaction (a shared resonator, or a direct capacitive link) that superconducting hardware can switch on for a precise duration to implement a specific two-qubit gate, then switch back off.

So the picture that started with a single Bloch vector precessing extends cleanly: single-qubit gates are time evolution under an isolated Hamiltonian (this lesson), two-qubit gates are time evolution under a coupling Hamiltonian, and Hardware Lab's chip layout is, among other things, a map of exactly which qubits have that coupling switched into existence at all.
