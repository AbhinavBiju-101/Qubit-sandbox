1

Two versions of one equation

The time-independent Schrödinger equation finds a system's fixed energy levels — for a qubit, that's just |0⟩ and |1⟩ again, now called energy eigenstates. The time-dependent version, written out in Step 2, says how a state changes moment to moment: each energy eigenstate spins in the complex plane at a rate set by its energy, e^{-iEt/ħ}.

Here's the part that matters for what you'll actually see: if a qubit is sitting purely in |0⟩ or purely in |1⟩, that spinning is an invisible global phase — nothing observable moves. It's only a superposition of the two — different energies spinning at different rates, drifting apart — that shows up as motion on the Bloch sphere. That drift is called precession.

This is the same phase gate from Complex Numbers, just now running continuously instead of by a slider you set once.
2

The equation itself, and where the spinning phase comes from

Worth actually writing the equation this lesson is named after, once, in full: iħ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩, where H is the Hamiltonian — the operator (matrix, for a qubit) representing total energy, and d|ψ⟩/dt is just how fast the state vector is changing, exactly as dx/dt means "velocity" for ordinary motion. It says: a state's rate of change is entirely determined by applying the energy operator to it — energy isn't just a number a state has, it's the very thing that drives its evolution forward in time.

Solving it for a single energy eigenstate |E⟩ — one where H|E⟩ = E|E⟩, meaning applying H just multiplies by the number E — is genuinely simple calculus, not new physics. Write the evolving state as c(t)|E⟩ for some time-dependent number c(t), substitute into the equation, and the |E⟩ cancels from both sides, leaving iħ · dc/dt = E · c(t) — an ordinary first-order differential equation for a single number. Rearranging: dc/dt = (-iE/ħ) · c(t), which is the textbook "rate of change proportional to the current value" equation — the same form as exponential growth or radioactive decay, except the constant of proportionality here is imaginary. Its solution is:

c(t) = c(0) · e^{-iEt/ħ}

That's the whole derivation behind Step 1's claim: an energy eigenstate doesn't just "happen" to acquire a spinning phase — it's forced to by the same calculus that governs any quantity whose rate of change is proportional to itself, just with an imaginary rather than real proportionality constant. An imaginary rate of change is precisely what turns exponential growth into rotation instead — recall from Complex Numbers, Step 2 that multiplying by a magnitude-1 complex number only ever rotates, never grows or shrinks, and |e^{-iEt/ħ}| = 1 for any real E and t. Energy doesn't make amplitudes grow or decay; it makes them spin, at a rate set by exactly how much energy there is.

4

Rabi flopping — how a real qubit gets controlled

Drive a qubit with a resonant pulse and, instead of precessing around the pole, it rotates all the way from |0⟩ to |1⟩ and back — a continuous version of the single-click H/X gates from Module 1. This is literally how Physical Qubit's "how it's controlled" step works: a microwave pulse plays the role of Ω below.

5

Where two-qubit gates actually come from

Everything above evolved one qubit under its own Hamiltonian. A CNOT or CZ gate between two qubits comes from exactly the same equation, just with a Hamiltonian that couples them — a term describing how each qubit's energy depends on the other's state. Physically, that coupling is usually a tunable interaction (a shared resonator, or a direct capacitive link) that superconducting hardware can switch on for a precise duration to implement a specific two-qubit gate, then switch back off.

So the picture that started with a single Bloch vector precessing extends cleanly: single-qubit gates are time evolution under an isolated Hamiltonian (this lesson), two-qubit gates are time evolution under a coupling Hamiltonian, and Hardware Lab's chip layout is, among other things, a map of exactly which qubits have that coupling switched into existence at all.
