Move a qubit's state without moving the qubit.

1

The four-move protocol

Alice has a qubit in some state she doesn't even need to know — call it the "secret." She and Bob also share an entangled Bell pair from earlier (Two Qubits, Step 5). The protocol: (1) Alice entangles her secret with her half of the pair, (2) she measures both of her qubits, getting two classical bits, (3) she sends those two bits to Bob over an ordinary channel, (4) Bob applies a small correction — X, Z, both, or neither, depending on the bits — and his qubit becomes the secret.

Nothing traveled faster than light: Bob's qubit isn't usable until Alice's two classical bits physically arrive — this is the same no-communication guarantee from Two Qubits, Step 6, doing real work here instead of just being a reassurance. What "teleports" is the state, not information on its own — and Alice's original qubit is left randomized in the process. Step 4 makes precise why that last part isn't optional.

The two classical bits Bob needs are exactly the two measurement outcomes from Alice's Bell-basis measurement — same four possible outcomes as the four Bell states from Two Qubits, just used here to steer a correction instead of being the answer themselves.
2

The algebra behind why it works

Step 1 asserted that Bob's correction is determined by Alice's two measured bits. Worth actually deriving that instead of taking it on faith — it's the single calculation that makes the whole protocol click. Start with the secret α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ on Alice's first qubit, and the shared Bell pair (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 across Alice's second qubit and Bob's qubit. Multiplying these out (the tensor product from Two Qubits, Step 2, now with three qubits) gives an 8-term, 3-qubit state:

(α|0⟩+β|1⟩) ⊗ (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 = (α|000⟩ + α|011⟩ + β|100⟩ + β|111⟩) / √2

That's a valid but unilluminating way to write it — the trick is to regroup Alice's two qubits (the first two kets) into the four Bell states instead of the four computational-basis states. Every 2-qubit state can be rewritten in either basis (this is exactly a change of measurement basis, the idea from Lesson 0, Step 6, now applied to a pair instead of a single qubit). Doing that regrouping — algebra you can expand yourself below — reorganizes the exact same 8 terms into 4 groups, one per Bell outcome, each multiplying a specific version of Bob's qubit:

Substituting |00⟩ = (|Φ+⟩+|Φ-⟩)/√2, |11⟩ = (|Φ+⟩−|Φ-⟩)/√2 for Alice's qubits when Bob's qubit is |0⟩, and |00⟩→(|Φ+⟩+|Φ-⟩)/√2 paired with a Bob |1⟩ from the α|011⟩ term, etc., and collecting every term that multiplies each of the four 2-qubit Bell states, the whole 8-term expression regroups exactly into:
½ [ |Φ+⟩(α|0⟩+β|1⟩) + |Φ-⟩(α|0⟩−β|1⟩) + |Ψ+⟩(α|1⟩+β|0⟩) + |Ψ-⟩(α|1⟩−β|0⟩) ]
with a global 1/√2 · 1/√2 = 1/2 out front matching the normalization of 4 equally-likely 25% outcomes.

This is the whole engine of the protocol, laid bare: Alice's measurement doesn't create Bob's state from nothing — it reveals, via which of the 4 equally-likely Bell outcomes she got, exactly which fixed, known transformation stands between whatever Bob already effectively has and the original secret. Bob was secretly holding a scrambled version of the secret the entire time (a consequence of the entanglement from Two Qubits), and the two classical bits are just the address of which scramble to undo — never a description of α and β themselves.

4

Bonus: why Alice can't peek first — and can't keep a copy either

If Alice measured her secret directly to "read" it before teleporting, she'd collapse it (Lesson 1, Step 5) and could only send classical bits describing one measured outcome — not the full complex state. Teleportation works precisely because Alice never learns α and β; she measures a joint property of her two qubits instead (Step 2's Bell-basis measurement), which is enough to steer Bob's correction without ever revealing the secret itself.

There's a second, related question worth asking: could Alice keep her original qubit untouched and also send Bob a copy — getting two working copies of the secret out of one? Physically, no, and this is important enough to have a name: the no-cloning theorem. It says no quantum operation can take an unknown state and produce two independent copies of it. The argument is short: if a cloning operation existed, it would have to work on every possible input state, including superpositions — but a machine that correctly copies |0⟩→|0⟩|0⟩ and |1⟩→|1⟩|1⟩ can be shown (by linearity — the fact that quantum operations must act the same way on a superposition as on its parts individually) to not correctly copy |+⟩ into |+⟩|+⟩; it produces an entangled mess instead. No consistent rule can clone every state, so cloning simply isn't a legal quantum operation, full stop.

Teleportation obeys this rule exactly rather than sneaking around it: watch the sandbox above closely and notice Alice's original secret qubit is destroyed by her Bell measurement in Step 3, in the very same click that produces the two bits Bob needs. There is never a moment where two working copies of the secret exist simultaneously — the state moves, it does not duplicate. This is also why the "teleportation" name, despite sounding like science fiction, is honest: like moving an object rather than faxing it, the original genuinely stops being in its original state.
5

Where this actually happens outside a browser tab

Teleportation is the basic building block of a future quantum internet — not for sending messages (classical bits still do that faster and cheaper, and Step 1's classical channel is required either way), but for moving quantum states between physically separate quantum processors so they can act as one larger computer, or for extending entanglement across long distances via a chain of quantum repeaters that each teleport entanglement one hop further than a single optical fiber could carry it undegraded.

It's also been demonstrated for real, well beyond a lab bench: China's Micius satellite teleported photon states to a ground station across more than a thousand kilometers in 2017, and it's been repeated between separate superconducting chips on a single circuit board — the same protocol you just ran, just with harder engineering around Steps 1–3 instead of a click.

Every one of those real demonstrations still needs a classical channel to send Alice's two bits — the physics you learned in Step 1 isn't a simplification for this app, it's the actual bottleneck research teams are engineering around. And every one of them destroys the source qubit exactly as Step 4 requires — the no-cloning theorem isn't a rule anyone gets to relax with cleverer hardware.
