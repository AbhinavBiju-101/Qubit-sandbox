MODULE 4 · WHERE QUANTUM COMES FROM

The curve that forced quantization.

1

The ultraviolet catastrophe

Any hot object glows across a spread of wavelengths — that spread is its blackbody spectrum. Classical physics (the Rayleigh-Jeans law) predicted that spectrum should keep rising forever as wavelength shrinks toward ultraviolet — meaning a warm object should radiate infinite energy. Real objects obviously don't do that.

Planck's fix, in 1900, was a hypothesis with no classical justification at the time: an oscillator can only emit or absorb energy in discrete packets, E = hf, never a continuous amount. That single assumption tames the curve exactly where experiment says it should bend over — and h, Planck's constant, has been load-bearing in physics ever since.

"Energy comes in discrete packets" is the same idea as "a qubit only ever measures 0 or 1" — Planck's hypothesis is discreteness showing up for energy, a century before it showed up for you as a qubit.

3

Bonus: why the classical curve explodes

The classical Rayleigh-Jeans law has no h in it at all — nothing stops short wavelengths from contributing more and more energy, without limit. Planck's law divides by e^{hc/λkT} − 1 instead, and for small λ that denominator explodes fast enough to crush the curve back to zero. One constant, one division, no more catastrophe.

4

Add up the whole curve: the Stefan-Boltzmann law

Wien's law (Step 2) tells you where the curve peaks. Integrating the entire Planck curve — every wavelength's contribution, added up — gives the total power radiated per unit area: P = σT⁴, with σ a fixed constant. That fourth power is dramatic: double an object's absolute temperature and it radiates 16 times the power, not twice.
