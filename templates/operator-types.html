Three definitions, in matrix arithmetic

Every matrix in quantum mechanics falls into a small number of named categories, and which one a given matrix belongs to tells you exactly what job it's allowed to do. M† means "conjugate-transpose M" — flip it across its diagonal and conjugate every entry (exactly the bra-from-ket operation, applied to a whole matrix instead of one vector).

Hermitian (M† = M) matrices are the ones that represent observables — anything you could physically measure, like the Z operator from Measurement & Stern-Gerlach. The defining reason this property matters: a theorem guarantees every Hermitian matrix has only real eigenvalues. That's not a coincidence — it's exactly why a measurement never hands you an imaginary number back. Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors, next lesson, is built entirely on this fact.

Unitary (M†M = I) matrices are the ones that represent gates — anything that evolves a state forward in time, like every H/X/Y/Z button you've ever clicked. The defining reason: unitary matrices are exactly the matrices that preserve a state's total probability at 1 no matter what they do to it. A gate that wasn't unitary would let probabilities leak away or exceed 100%, which isn't physically meaningful — so "is it a valid gate?" and "is it unitary?" are the same question.

Projection (M² = M) matrices represent measurement outcomes themselves — like |0⟩⟨0|, the operator that "asks" whether a state is |0⟩. Applying a projector twice does exactly what applying it once did, which is the algebraic way of saying "a state that's already collapsed onto an outcome stays collapsed onto it" — measuring an already-measured qubit again, in the same basis, just confirms the same answer.

HermitianM† = M — observables, real eigenvalues guaranteed.
UnitaryM†M = I — gates, probability always preserved.
ProjectionM² = M — measurement, idempotent by construction.
A matrix can be more than one at once — Z is both Hermitian and unitary, which is exactly why it can be measured (as an observable) or applied (as a gate). Those aren't two coincidentally-similar matrices; they're the literal same one, doing two different jobs depending how you use it.

Bonus: the odd one out

[[1,1],[0,1]] is neither Hermitian, unitary, nor a projection — which is exactly why it never shows up as a gate or an observable anywhere in this app. Every legitimate quantum gate has to be unitary; a matrix that isn't simply doesn't correspond to any physically realizable operation on a qubit.

4

Hermitian and unitary are both special cases of one bigger idea

Both Hermitian and unitary matrices share one deeper property: they commute with their own conjugate-transpose — MM† = M†M. Any matrix with that property is called normal, and it's the actual condition a theorem (the spectral theorem) needs to guarantee a clean set of orthonormal eigenvectors, the kind Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors relies on. Hermitian and unitary matrices are simply the two normal-matrix families that show up constantly in quantum mechanics — not a coincidence that both happen to diagonalize nicely, but a shared reason underneath.

The non-Hermitian example from Step 2, [[1,1],[0,1]], fails to be normal too — which is the deeper reason it couldn't be classified as anything useful: without normality, there's no guarantee of finding orthonormal eigenvectors for it at all.
