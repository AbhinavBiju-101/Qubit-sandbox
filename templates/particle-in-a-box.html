MODULE 4 · WHERE QUANTUM COMES FROM

Confine a wave, and only certain shapes fit.

Trap a particle between two walls and its wavefunction has to be zero at both ends — like a guitar string. Only a discrete ladder of wavelengths, and therefore energies, satisfies that. Five steps, including the full derivation and a genuine surprise at n=1.

1

Standing waves, not any wave

Confine a particle to a box of width L with infinitely hard walls, and its wavefunction must vanish at both walls — exactly the same constraint as a guitar string pinned at both ends. Only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit: λₙ = 2L/n for n = 1, 2, 3, ….

Through λ = h/p and E = p²/2m, that quantized wavelength ladder becomes a quantized energy ladder: Eₙ = n²h² / (8mL²). This is the simplest possible model that produces discrete energy levels from first principles — no hypothesis needed, just "the wave has to fit." Step 2 derives that formula in full.

Notice the shape of the formula: energy climbs as n², and as 1/L². Shrink the box and the levels spread apart fast — which is exactly why quantized energy is obvious for an electron confined to an atom, and utterly unmeasurable for a marble confined to a room.
2

Deriving Eₙ, and the surprise at n=1

Two formulas from de Broglie, chained together: the fitting condition λₙ = 2L/n, and momentum from wavelength, p = h/λ. Substitute one into the other:

pn = h/λn = h / (2L/n) = nh/(2L)

Then kinetic energy, E = p²/(2m):

En = (nh/2L)² / (2m) = n²h² / (8mL²)

Exactly Step 1's formula — nothing was asserted, only chained. Now notice what it says at the lowest possible level, n=1: E₁ = h²/(8mL²), a number that's small but never zero. A classical particle "trapped and at rest" would have exactly zero energy — sitting perfectly still. Quantum mechanically, that option simply isn't on the ladder; n=0 would mean λ=∞, a wave with no wiggles at all, which can't satisfy "zero at both walls" unless it's zero everywhere, which isn't a physical state. This unavoidable minimum, called zero-point energy, isn't a quirk of this one model — it's a direct consequence of the same uncertainty relation from Commutation Relations: confining a particle's position pins down Δx, and the uncertainty bound forces Δp (and therefore kinetic energy) to be strictly positive. A particle that's genuinely confined can never be genuinely at rest.

4

Bonus: degeneracy in a 2D box

Add a second dimension and the energy becomes E(nₓ,n_y) ∝ nₓ² + n_y². Different quantum numbers can now land on the exact same energy — that's called degeneracy, and it's not a coincidence when the box is square. This is the same word, and the same idea, as Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors, Step 5's identity matrix: one number (here, one energy) shared by more than one distinct state.

(1,2) & (2,1)both give nₓ²+n_y² = 5
(1,4) & (4,1) & (2, √14)†not all match — degeneracy depends on the exact numbers
(2,3) & (3,2)both give nₓ²+n_y² = 13

† only whole-number pairs count — that entry is there to show not every level has a partner, not every n has a degenerate twin.

5

Real systems this model actually predicts

Quantum dots — nanometer-scale semiconductor crystals — confine electrons closely enough that this exact model, box width and all, predicts their optical properties: shrink the dot and Eₙ ∝ 1/L² pushes the energy levels apart, which shifts the color of light the dot absorbs and emits. That's not a loose analogy; quantum-dot displays and biological imaging tags are engineered by tuning dot size using essentially this formula.

Chemists use the same model for conjugated molecules — long chains of alternating single/double bonds — treating the delocalized electrons as roughly free to move along the chain's length, confined at the ends. It's a simplification (a real molecule isn't a perfectly flat-bottomed box), but it gets the right qualitative answer: longer conjugated chains have more closely-spaced energy levels and absorb longer-wavelength light, exactly as 1/L² predicts.
