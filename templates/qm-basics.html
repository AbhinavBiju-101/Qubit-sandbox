The four numbers under everything else.

1

A state is a list of numbers, not a "thing"

In quantum mechanics, everything you could possibly measure about a system is packed into a state vector — literally a short list of numbers, one per possible outcome. For a qubit, there are exactly two possible measurement outcomes, conventionally labeled 0 and 1, so the state vector has exactly two entries:

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩

α and β are called probability amplitudes. |0⟩ and |1⟩ are just labels for "the outcome-0 slot" and "the outcome-1 slot" — not numbers themselves.

This is why quantum computing scales the way it does. This "space of all possible states" is called a Hilbert space, and its size is 2ⁿ for n qubits — 1 qubit needs 2 numbers, 2 qubits need 4 (see the Two Qubits lesson), 300 qubits would need more numbers than there are atoms in the observable universe. Every qubit you add doubles the list, it doesn't just add one more entry.

The "wave function" you may have heard of is the same idea in the more general, continuous case (position, momentum) — a qubit is the finite, two-outcome special case, which is exactly why it's the easiest place to start.
2

Amplitudes aren't probabilities — you have to square them

This is the single most important rule in this lesson, known as Born's rule: the probability of measuring outcome 0 is α², not α. (Real quantum mechanics uses complex α, β, and the rule is technically |α|² — squared magnitude — but restricting to real numbers here keeps the sliders honest without losing the point. Complex phase shows up starting in the Single Qubit lesson's Bloch sphere.)

Why "normalized" isn't optional

Probabilities have to add up to 100%, full stop — that's not a quantum thing, it's just what "probability" means. So α² + β² = 1 isn't a nice-to-have, it's a hard requirement on any valid state vector. Try feeding in a vector that violates it:

Normalizing is nothing exotic — divide every entry by the vector's length (√(α² + β²)), same as turning any vector into a unit vector. Every gate in the Single Qubit lesson is built to preserve normalization automatically, so you'll never need to do this by hand there — but now you know why it has to.

That's the whole prerequisite: a state is two numbers, probabilities are those numbers squared, and the squares have to sum to 1. Everything in the Single Qubit lesson — gates, the Bloch sphere, measurement — is built directly on these three facts, plus one addition: α and β are actually complex, which is what makes the Bloch sphere a sphere instead of a quarter-circle.
