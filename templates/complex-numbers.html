A number with two parts

A complex number z = a + bi is just a pair of real numbers, a (the real part) and b (the imaginary part), with i² = -1 as the one new rule. Every amplitude you've clicked into existence since Single Qubit — α and β — is one of these.

Two things about z matter for quantum mechanics far more than a and b individually: its magnitude |z| = √(a² + b²) (how far from zero) and its phase arg(z) (which direction, as an angle). Born's rule only ever uses |z|² — the phase is invisible to any single measurement, but it is not invisible to interference, which is where it does all its work.

Real numbers have a magnitude too (|−3| = 3), but only two directions: positive or negative. Complex numbers have a magnitude and a full 360° of direction. That extra dial — phase — is the thing amplitudes need that plain probabilities never did.

A phase real numbers couldn't hide

Apply H, then dial in a phase gate P(θ) = diag(1, e^{iθ}) — it multiplies only β by a point on the unit circle, leaving |α| and |β| completely alone. Watch the Bloch point spin around the equator while the measurement odds don't move at all.

State: —
50.0%P(measure 0)
50.0%P(measure 1)
Both probabilities stay pinned at 50/50 for every value of θ. If amplitudes were plain real numbers, "rotate the phase without touching the probabilities" wouldn't even be a sentence you could write — a real rotation is a probability change. That's the whole reason quantum states live in complex vector space.
